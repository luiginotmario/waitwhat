# Story arcs, with outlines

Choose the arc that fits the material; mix when needed. Each outline is a list of scene goals.

## Mystery → resolution (default for "why is X true?")
1. Show the surprising fact working on a concrete case.  2. State the question plainly.
3. A first naive idea, and why it is not enough.  4. Gather the one tool needed.
5. The aha: one visual moment that makes it inevitable.  6. Name it / write the formula.
7. Where else it shows up (surprising application).  8. Echo the opening picture.

*Example: "Why does the Pythagorean theorem work?"*
hook (three squares, "always equal, why?") → question (areas, not lengths) → four copies of the
triangle in a big square → rearrange: the leftover is a² + b² → rearrange again: the leftover is c²
→ same big square, same triangles, so a² + b² = c² → why "right" angle matters (it fails otherwise)
→ closing (any right triangle, forever).

## Build-up → payoff ("how does X work?")
Small, satisfying pieces stacked until the whole machine runs. Good for algorithms, engines,
the internet, neural networks. Show each piece doing its job on real data, then run the whole
thing at speed for the payoff.

## Two views → unity
Two descriptions that look unrelated (a sum and a shape, a wave and a particle, an equation and
a picture) shown separately, then morphed into each other. The transform *is* the aha.

## Specific → general
One fully worked example with real numbers, then vary the numbers (a ValueTracker sweep), then
reveal the pattern as a rule. Good for formulas, statistics, finance.

## Timeline (history, biography, "how did we get here?")
Not a list of dates: a chain of problems. Each era is a problem people faced, the idea that
solved it, and the new problem that idea created. Anchor everything on one persistent timeline.

## Cause → effect chain (science, economics, policy)
Start from an everyday observation, walk the causal chain one link per scene with a persistent
flow diagram that grows, then show a feedback loop or a surprising downstream effect.

## Founding story → how it works (companies, inventions, discoveries)
When the subject has a real origin story, open with it and hang the explanation on it.
1. The protagonist and the moment: who, where, what they were trying to do.
2. The pain, concrete: what it cost them (and everyone like them). This is the *but*.
3. What people did before, and why it was not enough.
4. The turning point: the insight or the hack, in one picture.
5. How it works, now that the viewer cares: the mechanism, as deep as the audience level needs.
6. What it grew into (only verifiable numbers), and one surprising consequence.
7. Echo the opening moment, changed by what we now know.

*Example: "What does stripe.com do?"* (outline shape only; verify every fact first)
two brothers trying to sell software online, and the weeks of paperwork a merchant account
took → why accepting a card is hard (the card network, the banks, a payment processor in between,
each term explained as it appears) → the turning point: a few lines of code instead of weeks →
follow one payment from "Pay" to the money arriving → how they make money → who uses it → back
to the developer, the button now one paste away.

For the *but*, use "and, but, therefore": situation and situation, **but** a tension, **therefore**
a consequence. If a topic has no real conflict, use a turn instead (introduce, develop, twist,
reconcile) rather than inventing one.

## Company explainer (see waitwhat:research for the research half)
1. An everyday problem, shown concretely (a person, a moment, a cost). If the founders' own
   story is documented, that person is the founder (see the founding-story arc above).
2. Why it is hard / what people do today.  3. What the company built, in one sentence.
4. A concrete example walked end to end (a real use case from their site).
5. How it works underneath (only as deep as helps).  6. How they make money.
7. Who uses it (only verifiable customers or categories).  8. Back to the person from scene 1.

## Paper / document explainer
The question the authors asked → why existing approaches fall short → the key idea, as a
picture → how they tested it → the result, with the real numbers from the paper → limitations
the authors name → why it matters. Quote figures exactly; label anything you simplified.
